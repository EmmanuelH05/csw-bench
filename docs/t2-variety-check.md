# T2 variety check — which Spanish word orders survive a Caribbean-influenced corpus

> **Status: Phase 1 finding, Track A/B collision. Not ratified.**
> Written against `taxonomy.md` § T2, `corpora.md` § 1, `limitations.md` § "Every attested
> item comes from one corpus".

## The collision

Two Phase 1 results meet here. (a) Licensing recon left the Bangor Miami Corpus as the sole
usable attested source: 84 Spanish/English bilinguals, Miami, 2008–2011, a
Caribbean-influenced speech community. (b) T2 requires probes where Spanish word order is
**obligatory**, and the ordering stress test (C15) already found one probe —
wh-question non-inversion — that is a violation in the standard and native in Caribbean
Spanish.

The question this document answers: after applying the "obligatory across all major
varieties" restriction to a Caribbean-influenced population, what is left of T2?

**Headline.** Preverbal clitic placement with finite verbs **survives the dialect test** —
no source found documents finite-verb enclisis in Cuban, Puerto Rican, or Dominican
Spanish. But it **fails T2's own diagnostic question 2** for a structural reason
independent of dialect, and the taxonomy's own constructed example demonstrates the
failure: `*Yo veo lo en la tienda` contains no EL material at all and is therefore a
monolingual word-order error, terminated at T2 question 2 (the C4 exclusion). Three of the
six candidate probes are dead on variety grounds, one is dead on optionality grounds
independent of variety, and the strongest survivor is not clitic placement but **nominal
possessor order**, which the taxonomy currently files under C9 as a contested T1/T2
overlap.

## 1. Probe-by-probe findings

| Probe | Obligatory in Caribbean Spanish? | Usable as a T2 probe? |
|---|---|---|
| Preverbal clitic, finite verb | **Yes** — no documented variation [V] | **No** — cannot form a mixed constituent [I] |
| Clitic climbing (finite + infinitive) | **No** — optional in the standard itself [V] | No |
| Wh-question S–V inversion | **No** — non-inversion native, quantified [V] | No (already rejected, C15) |
| Subject pronoun expression / position | **No** — Caribbean rates high, SV strongly preferred [V] | No |
| Negation placement (preverbal `no`) | **Yes** — retained even under Dominican doubling [V] | Marginal — low mixed-constituent yield [I] |
| Adjective placement | Not obligatory anywhere | No (rejection confirmed, and for the original reason) |
| **Nominal possessor order (N + `de` + possessor)** | **Yes** — no variety has a prenominal genitive [V/I] | **Yes** — but collides with C9 |
| Preposition pied-piping | Yes in monolingual grammar [V] | **Compromised** — this population accepts stranding [V] |
| No adverb/negator between clitic and finite verb | Yes [V] | Marginal — low yield [I] |

### 1.1 Preverbal clitic placement with finite verbs — the load-bearing case

**Dialect verdict: survives.** Ordóñez (*Clitics*, handbook chapter, Stony Brook ms.;
year not printed on the copy retrieved `[U: year]`) states that clitics must precede
tensed verbs in Spanish, with enclisis on non-finite forms and imperatives [V]. He further
establishes, with starred examples, that no adverb and no negator may intervene between
clitic and tensed verb (`*Lo no vio`, `*Lo nunca vio`), and that with an auxiliary the
clitic must move left of the auxiliary — the position between auxiliary and participle is
excluded [V]. Mackenzie's Spanish-linguistics reference pages (Newcastle University, n.d.;
not peer-reviewed) give the same rule with the escape hatch "except in archaic or dialectal
Spanish" [V].

That escape hatch does not open onto the Caribbean. The dialects with productive
finite-verb enclisis identified in the search are **Asturian and Asturian Spanish**, plus
Old Spanish and Western Ibero-Romance more broadly [S — from a lingref/WSS paper on
Asturian enclisis and a Cambridge *Journal of Linguistics* paper on Old Spanish V2, neither
read in full]. **I found no source documenting finite-verb enclisis in Cuban, Puerto Rican,
or Dominican Spanish** [V-negative]. Searches targeting Caribbean clitic variation return
work on *variable* clitic placement, which is a different phenomenon (see 1.2), and the
Caribbean-syntax surveys consulted (Mackenzie; Zimmermann 2019) list non-inversion, overt
and preverbal subjects, expletive *ello*, focalizing *es*, and double negation as the
distinctive features — clitic–verb order is not among them [V].

One residual uncertainty, marked because it touches clitics directly: Mackenzie reports
that the Caribbean focalizing *es* construction permits clitic promotion unavailable in
standard pseudo-clefts [V]. That is a claim about the *domain* of clitic movement, not
about proclisis to a finite verb, but it is the one place where Caribbean grammar is
reported to differ in clitic behaviour, and any item built near a focalizing *es* should be
excluded. `[VERIFY: wording — Mackenzie is a web reference, not a peer-reviewed source]`

**T2 verdict: fails anyway, and not for dialect reasons.** This is the substantive
finding. T2 question 2 requires the constituent to contain EL material; question 3 requires
it not to be an EL island. Spanish clitic placement is a relation between a system morpheme
(the clitic) and a finite verb. In a Spanish-ML clause both are ML by construction, so the
constituent whose order is being evaluated has no place for EL material [I]. The three ways
to force EL material in all fail:

1. **Bare EL verb** — `*Yo watch lo`. Fires T5's diagnostic (EL content morpheme lacking
   obligatory ML morphology) as well as T2's. First-match ordering awards it to T2, but the
   violation an annotator perceives is the bare form, not the order. Construct-invalid.
2. **Morphologically integrated loanverb** — `*Yo watcheo lo`. An integrated form is
   plausibly ML material under MLF's own logic, which puts us back at case 0: no EL
   material [I]. `[VERIFY: the literature's treatment of integrated loanverbs' ML status —
   this is exactly the judgment `taxonomy.md` already flags for a second reviewer]`
3. **EL participle under a Spanish auxiliary** — `*He watcheado lo`. Structurally the
   cleanest: proclisis to the auxiliary is obligatory [V], and the EL stem sits inside the
   VP. Still inherits problem 2's question about the stem's ML status.

And the taxonomy's own example is case 0. `*Yo veo lo en la tienda` is entirely Spanish.
Under the diagnostic as currently written it is terminated at question 2 as a monolingual
word-order error — the C4 exclusion, added by the stress test, retroactively invalidates
the type's headline example. **This is a ratification blocker independent of everything
else in this document.**

### 1.2 Clitic climbing — dead on optionality, not on dialect

Ordóñez shows the climbing clitic may surface in the matrix position, in any intermediate
position, or on the final infinitive, in restructuring, causative, and perception-verb
sequences alike [V]. Mackenzie describes *Quieren comprarlo* / *Lo quieren comprar* as
stylistic alternatives rather than dialectal variants [V]. The variationist literature
treats placement here as a genuine variable conditioned by finite-verb lexeme and object
animacy (*A Usage-Based Perspective on Spanish Variable Clitic Placement*, **Languages**
5(3), 2020 — `[U: author]`, MDPI blocked retrieval; findings taken from search-result
summary, so **[S]**), with a reported ~25% enclisis rate for adult Latin American varieties
`[U: source of that figure]`.

Optional in the standard means it can never satisfy T2 question 5. Dialect is irrelevant.
**Reject.**

### 1.3 Wh-question subject–verb inversion — dead, and now quantified

Confirms C15 with numbers. Brown & Rivas (2011, "Subject–verb word order in Spanish
interrogatives: A quantitative analysis of Puerto Rican Spanish", *Spanish in Context* 8(1))
report, over 882 wh-questions of conversational Puerto Rican Spanish, non-inversion in 14%
of cases and 25% of wh-questions containing an overt subject, alongside 51% subject
expression and a 47%-SV / 4%-VS split across sentence types [S — figures from the search
summary and the John Benjamins listing; the full text 403'd, so treat the numbers as
**secondhand** until the PDF is read]. Zimmermann (2019, "Reconsidering the syntax of
interrogatives in Caribbean Spanish, with special reference to Dominican Spanish",
*Isogloss* 5) analyses whSV as the product of ongoing parametric change — a weak
subject-pronoun paradigm, a dedicated SpecTP, and a general SV drift — rather than as an
exception [V, abstract read]. A variationist study across Puerto Rican, Cuban, and Dominican
speech reports epistemic verbs and singular subjects favouring non-inversion (LSRL 49
abstract, author "Guo" — `[U: full citation]`, abstract-level only, **[S]**).

**Reject.** Already rejected; this section exists so the rejection cites something.

### 1.4 Subject pronoun expression and position — dead, and doubly so

Two independent strikes.

*Rate.* Otheguy & Zentella's New York work (*Spanish in New York: Language Contact,
Dialectal Leveling, and Structural Continuity*, 2012 `[U: year]`; and Otheguy, Zentella &
Livert, *Language* `[U: year]`) finds Caribbean overt-pronoun rates significantly higher
than Mainland rates, and rising further with time in the US contact setting, across a
corpus of ~63,500 verbs from 142 speakers [S — figures from search summaries; neither work
read in full]. Miami is a US contact setting with a Caribbean-majority Spanish population,
so both effects point the same way [I].

*Position.* Brown & Rivas' 47%/4% SV-over-VS split covers all sentence types, not only
interrogatives [S]. Caribbean Spanish additionally licenses **preverbal subjects of
infinitives** — *para yo entenderlo*, *antes de tú venir aquí* — where other varieties
require a postverbal subject or a finite subjunctive clause [V, Mackenzie; corroborated by
search summaries reporting 100% speaker acceptance of preverbal infinitival subjects
`[U: source]`]. That is precisely an English-looking S–V order that is independently
licensed in the variety, which is T2 question 5's failure mode in its purest form.

**Reject**, and note the asymmetry: this probe is not merely unusable, it is a source of
**false positives** — an annotator applying standard-Spanish intuitions to Miami data will
flag native constructions as violations.

### 1.5 Negation placement — survives, low yield

Lipski (2001 ms., "Strategies of double negation in Spanish and Portuguese"; the copy
retrieved carries a *do not cite without permission* notice — **clear this before citing in
a published artifact**) states that Spanish negation varies remarkably little across the
Spanish-speaking world [V]. Dominican double negation is `no V ... no` — the **preverbal
negator is retained**, the postposed one is additive [V, from the Dominican examples
listed]. Cuban `no ... no` is reported as extremely scarce in spoken Cuban Spanish [V].
The vernacular Caribbean `no ... ná(da)` pattern likewise keeps preverbal `no` [V].
Strictly postverbal negation appears in Chocó Spanish and Palenquero — not in the Caribbean
varieties feeding Miami [V].

So **preverbal `no` is obligatory across the relevant varieties**, and so is the fixed
`no > CL > V` ordering [V, Ordóñez]. The problem is yield, not validity: constructing a
mixed constituent where English imposes a different negator position generally means
importing an English negator, which makes the item an EL system morpheme case (widened T1)
that T2 only wins by ordering precedence [I]. Usable, but not in volume.

### 1.6 Adjective placement — rejection confirmed

Nothing in the Caribbean literature surveyed alters the taxonomy's reason for excluding it:
both orders are licensed in Spanish with a meaning difference, so it fails T2 question 4/5
in every variety. The Caribbean data neither rescues nor further damages it. **Confirmed
reject**, for the original reason.

### 1.7 New candidates found

**Nominal possessor order — the strongest survivor.** Spanish has no prenominal genitive;
possession is N + `de` + possessor. No Caribbean-specific prenominal-genitive construction
appears in any survey consulted [V-negative], and none is plausible — this is a
core-grammar property, not a variable [I]. A mixed constituent is easy to build
(`*Mary's libro` inside a Spanish frame), EL material is present by construction, and a
single English possessor name is a poor island candidate. This is the only probe that
satisfies all five T2 questions cleanly.

Its cost: `taxonomy.md` files English possessive `'s` as **C9**, resolved to T2 by the
council but flagged for **re-adjudication under the widened T1**, where `'s` is a late
bridge system morpheme and hence now admissible to T1. If C9 re-adjudicates to T1, T2's
best remaining probe leaves with it. **T2's viability is therefore contingent on the C9
re-adjudication, which is currently an open ratification item.** That dependency is not
recorded anywhere in `taxonomy.md` and should be.

**Preposition pied-piping — compromised by the population, not the dialect.**
Koronkiewicz (2022, "Preposition stranding in Spanish–English code-switching", *Languages*
7(1)) confirms pied-piping as the Spanish norm and stranding as the English one, then finds
that **simultaneous bilinguals accepted p-stranding across both languages and all
code-switching conditions**, while child sequential bilinguals kept the monolingual
distinction [V, abstract read; n = 24]. Bangor Miami is a US heritage-bilingual population
that certainly includes simultaneous bilinguals. Same failure shape as C15 — a construction
obligatory in the reference grammar but licensed for the actual speakers — arriving through
bilingualism rather than through regional dialect. **This generalizes the C15 problem: T2's
"obligatory across all major Spanish varieties" restriction is not strong enough. The
operative population is Caribbean-influenced *US heritage* Spanish, and the restriction must
read "obligatory for that population."** [I]

**Clitic cluster order and clitic–verb adjacency.** Both obligatory [V, Ordóñez;
Mackenzie]. Both nearly impossible to instantiate with EL material inside the relevant
constituent [I]. Recorded for completeness, not counted.

## 2. What this means for the 100-item quota

Two distinct constraints, worth separating because `taxonomy.md` currently blurs them.

**Validity.** Every rejected probe above was rejected because the "violation" is or may be
grammatical for the Miami population. This is not fixable by finding more data; it is fixable
only by choosing different probes. Applies to constructed and attested items alike.

**Supply.** Even for surviving probes, attested *violations* are rare by hypothesis — MLF
predicts they should not occur, so a 242k-word naturalistic corpus is not a reservoir of
them. If T2 items are predominantly constructed minimal pairs with the corpus supplying
naturalistic grammatical material and the one required attested example, supply is not the
binding constraint and validity is. If T2 items must be attested violations, T2 cannot reach
100 by any probe. `taxonomy.md` does not state which regime applies; **it must, before this
question can be answered numerically.** [I]

Under the constructed-item regime, the honest arithmetic on the surviving inventory:

| Probe | Realistic share of ~100 |
|---|---|
| Nominal possessor order | Most of it — **if C9 stays with T2** |
| Negation / `no > CL > V` ordering | Small |
| Clitic–verb adjacency, cluster order | Marginal |
| Preverbal clitic placement (auxiliary + EL participle) | Small, and contested on the loanverb-status question |

A type whose item pool is one probe plus a tail is a type built on a single construction.
Held out by `matriz`, it would test whether a model has learned *English possessive order
inside Spanish NPs*, not whether it has represented the Morpheme Order Principle. That is a
construct-validity failure of the same family as the single-pair concern already in
`limitations.md`. [I]

## 3. Verdict

**Preverbal clitic placement survives as a fact about Caribbean Spanish and fails as a T2
probe.** No source documents finite-verb enclisis in Cuban, Puerto Rican, or Dominican
Spanish, so the dialect worry is answered in T2's favour. But the probe cannot produce a
mixed constituent, T2 question 2 excludes it, and the taxonomy's own headline example is a
monolingual string that its own diagnostic rejects.

**T2 cannot be populated to ~100 items as currently specified.** Its stated probe inventory
— "clitic placement, some interrogative structures, and negation" — is, after this check:
one probe unusable, one dead, one low-yield. What is left is a probe the taxonomy files
under a different type pending re-adjudication.

Three options, in preference order:

1. **Re-scope T2 around nominal possessor order and resolve C9 to T2 explicitly**,
   documenting that T2 is effectively a one-construction type and accepting the
   construct-validity cost in `limitations.md`. Keeps four types. Cheapest, and honest only
   if the narrowness is written down rather than implied.
2. **Widen T2's probe criterion from "obligatory across varieties" to "obligatory for
   Caribbean-influenced US heritage Spanish", then re-survey.** The p-stranding finding
   shows the current criterion is aimed at the wrong population; a re-survey against the
   right one might surface probes this pass missed. Costs a Phase 1 extension with an
   uncertain result.
3. **Drop T2. Ship three types.** Breaches the four-type minimum in `docs/charter.md`. Stated
   plainly because the evidence genuinely permits it: T2 has now failed on populability
   twice, and the T4 precedent — "a type `matriz` cannot meaningfully hold out is not a
   type, it is a rounding error with a name" — applies to a type reduced to one
   construction just as it applied to a type with no clean probes.

My recommendation is **1 with 2 attempted first**, and an explicit ratification blocker
added: T2 cannot be ratified while its constructed example fails its own question 2 and
while its best probe's type assignment is open.

## 5. Explicitly not established

- **Bangor Miami's speaker composition is unverified.** Miami's Spanish-speaking population
  is Cuban-majority, and the corpus documentation describes post-recording demographic
  questionnaires, but **I did not verify the national-origin breakdown of the 84 speakers**.
  `limitations.md` and this document both currently assert "Caribbean-influenced" on the
  basis of the recording location alone. The corpus metadata can settle it and should —
  the entire argument here is calibrated to a speaker population nobody has counted.
- **Whether morphologically integrated EL loanverbs count as ML material** under MLF. This
  decides whether probe-case 2 in §1.1 is usable at all.
- **The `Languages` 5(3) VCP paper's authorship**, and the provenance of the 25%
  Latin-American enclisis figure.
- **Whether any Caribbean variety shows clitic–finite-verb order variation** beyond the
  focalizing-*es* promotion effect. Absence of evidence in ten targeted searches is
  suggestive, not conclusive; a Caribbean-syntax handbook chapter would settle it, and the
  one located (*Caribbean Spanish and Theoretical Syntax: An Overview*, Academia.edu) was
  behind a 403.
